## 1. Purpose

Aurex must begin with a real, testable problem rather than a predetermined product. The sequence is:

```text
Real-world problem -> Root causes -> Requirements -> Possible solutions -> Validation
```

Kenya is the initial case because it combines strong renewable-electricity progress with continuing challenges in affordability, reliability, efficiency, data, coordination, and infrastructure modernization.

The founding rationale is set out in the [Founding Thesis](./FOUNDING-THESIS.md). This document narrows that rationale into a problem that can be researched, measured, and tested.

## 2. Context

Kenya's electricity system is becoming more complex. Renewable generation, distributed solar, batteries, mini-grids, backup systems, electric mobility, flexible loads, digital meters, commercial demand, and new energy services are developing alongside existing utility and network infrastructure.

This transition creates greater need for:

- Reliable and affordable electricity service
- Renewable forecasting and system flexibility
- Visibility into facilities and distributed energy resources
- Interoperability across devices, data, and institutions
- Better loss, outage, demand, and asset intelligence
- Secure, governed, and accountable digital operations

Continued investment in generation, transmission, distribution, maintenance, regulation, finance, and human capacity remains essential. Software cannot replace those foundations. The question is how a complementary digital coordination layer can help existing and future investments perform better.

Supporting energy context and current evidence should be maintained through the [Research & Innovation Framework](./RESEARCH-AND-INNOVATION-FRAMEWORK.md), not repeated here as a static catalogue of sector statistics.

## 3. Root Problem

> **Kenya's electricity system is becoming more renewable, distributed, data-dependent, and demand-sensitive, but its coordination architecture has not yet matured enough to make relevant assets, facilities, data, institutions, and users sufficiently visible, interoperable, flexible, secure, and economically optimized.**

This is a structural problem, not a claim that any single institution has failed. It spans physical infrastructure, data, software, institutions, governance, and economics.

## 4. Problem Areas

The root problem appears in five connected areas:

### Visibility

Energy actors may lack timely, usable information about distributed assets, facility demand, network conditions, outages, equipment health, flexible loads, and available resilience resources.

### Interoperability

Meters, inverters, batteries, building systems, utility platforms, outage systems, GIS, SCADA, forecasting tools, and analytics systems may use different interfaces, data models, permissions, and operating processes.

### Facility performance

Facilities experience energy as cost, uptime, equipment risk, productivity, comfort, and safety, yet many lack practical tools to monitor, forecast, optimize, and report their energy use.

### System flexibility and efficiency

A more renewable and distributed system needs better coordination of storage, flexible demand, electric-vehicle charging, local constraints, outages, and loss-reduction interventions. The ability to create value from these resources must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

### Institutional trust

Digital energy systems require clear authority, data permissions, cybersecurity, privacy, safety boundaries, accountability, and public-interest safeguards. Open technology must be compatible with regulated operations.

The technical and security implications are developed in the [Security & Responsible Technology Policy](./SECURITY-AND-RESPONSIBLE-TECHNOLOGY-POLICY.md) and [Open-Source & Licensing Framework](./OPEN-SOURCE-AND-LICENSING-FRAMEWORK.md). Institutional safeguards are defined in the [Governance Framework](./GOVERNANCE-FRAMEWORK.md), [Ethics, Sovereignty & Public-Interest Framework](./ETHICS-SOVEREIGNTY-AND-PUBLIC-INTEREST-FRAMEWORK.md), and [Legal & Institutional Readiness](./LEGAL-AND-INSTITUTIONAL-READINESS.md).

## 5. Initial Problem Boundary

Aurex's initial research boundary includes:

- Facility-level energy visibility and optimization
- Open tools for energy monitoring, modeling, and decision support
- Interoperability patterns for energy data and devices
- Distributed-energy visibility and coordination research
- Demand-side intelligence and flexibility validation
- Loss and outage analytics
- Energy-data governance and cybersecurity design
- Evidence from research, prototypes, pilots, standards, and partnerships

It excludes acting as a national utility, regulator, or system operator; claiming government approval; replacing physical grid investment; or exercising operational control without authority, validation, and cybersecurity readiness.

The staged technical pathway is described in the [Founding Thesis](./FOUNDING-THESIS.md) and [Operating Model](./OPERATING-MODEL.md).

## 6. Official Statement

Kenya's electricity system is entering a phase in which renewable generation, distributed energy resources, facility-level systems, electric mobility, growing demand, network constraints, affordability pressure, reliability expectations, and institutional modernization must be coordinated with greater precision than legacy operating models can easily support.

Kenya therefore needs continued physical and institutional investment together with a complementary digital coordination layer that improves visibility, interoperability, forecasting, facility-level intelligence, demand-side insight, flexibility management, loss and outage analysis, cybersecurity, and evidence-based decision-making.

Aurex Digital Solutions is being established to research, develop, validate, and steward open digital energy coordination infrastructure that can help Kenya, and later other African energy systems, translate renewable-energy potential and infrastructure investment into reliable, resilient, affordable, transparent, and productive energy service.

## 7. Validation Questions

Aurex must test, rather than assume, the following:

- Which Kenyan facility types have the strongest need and ability to adopt energy-management tools?
- What minimum data and telemetry are required to produce useful decisions at acceptable cost?
- Can open-source tools deliver measurable cost, efficiency, reliability, or resilience benefits while maintaining security?
- Which interoperability patterns work across realistic Kenyan devices, systems, and institutions?
- Which distributed resources can provide useful flexibility, and under what technical and regulatory conditions?
- Can digital tools help identify, prioritize, and measure loss- or outage-reduction interventions?
- What data permissions, privacy controls, safety boundaries, and accountability arrangements are required?
- Which outcomes should pilots measure, and what evidence would justify expanding beyond facility-level work?
- Where is software valuable, and where are physical infrastructure, financing, or regulatory changes the binding constraint?

The research design, evidence standards, and pilot learning process belong in the [Research & Innovation Framework](./RESEARCH-AND-INNOVATION-FRAMEWORK.md), [Risk Management Framework](./RISK-MANAGEMENT-FRAMEWORK.md), and [Programme Governance Framework](./PROGRAMME-GOVERNANCE-FRAMEWORK.md).

## 8. One-Sentence Version

Kenya's electricity system is becoming more renewable, distributed, and demand-sensitive, but it lacks a mature open digital coordination layer to make energy assets, facilities, data, institutions, and users sufficiently visible, interoperable, flexible, secure, and economically optimized.
