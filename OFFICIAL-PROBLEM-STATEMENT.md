> This document defines the problem that Aurex Digital Solutions exists to investigate, develop for, validate, and steward. It does not claim that Aurex Digital Solutions is a government agency, a licensed utility, a regulator, a national grid operator, or an already deployed national energy platform.

## 1. Purpose

The purpose of this document is to define the structural energy-system problem that justifies the formation of Aurex Digital Solutions.

The document deliberately begins with the real-world problem rather than with a predetermined technology answer. Aurex Digital Solutions should not begin by saying "Kenya needs a DERMS" or "Africa needs an Energy Operating System." Those statements assume the solution before the problem has been properly defined.

The sequence must be:

```text
Real Energy Problem
        |
Root Causes
        |
System Requirements
        |
Possible Solutions
        |
Validation
        |
Technology
```

The institutional question is:

> What structural problem in Kenya's emerging electricity system requires a new digital coordination capability?

Kenya is used as the initial case study because it combines strong renewable-electricity progress with persistent affordability, reliability, system-efficiency, data, coordination, and infrastructure-modernization challenges.

## 2. Kenya Energy Context

Kenya has made substantial progress in electricity development.

Public energy analysis identifies Kenya as a regional leader in renewable electricity generation, electricity-access expansion, geothermal development, and clean-energy policy ambition. The International Energy Agency's Kenya 2024 review reports that Kenya's electricity generation mix was nearly 90% renewable in 2023, led by geothermal, hydro, wind, and solar. The same review states that electricity access increased from 37% in 2013 to 79% in 2023 and that Kenya is on track to reach universal electricity access by 2030.

Kenya's energy-transition ambition is also reflected in the Kenya Energy Transition and Investment Plan, which frames a long-term pathway for renewable power, clean cooking, transport electrification, hydrogen, industry, and broader decarbonization.

At the same time, Kenya's electricity system continues to face important constraints:

- Electricity remains expensive for many households and enterprises.
- Distribution and transmission networks still face high technical and commercial losses.
- Outages and quality-of-supply problems continue to affect customers.
- Demand is growing as access, industry, digital services, mobility, and productive electricity use expand.
- Variable renewable energy increases the need for flexibility, forecasting, storage, operational coordination, and grid services.
- Energy data is improving but remains insufficiently structured, complete, timely, and accessible for modern coordination needs.
- Institutional coordination across power, transport, industry, buildings, counties, regulators, utilities, developers, facilities, and consumers is increasingly important.

This context creates Kenya's energy paradox:

> Kenya can be both a renewable-electricity leader and a country that still needs deeper reliability, affordability, efficiency, flexibility, data, and coordination improvements.

The problem is therefore not a simple absence of generation. New generation, transmission, and distribution investment remain necessary. But physical infrastructure alone cannot solve every operational coordination problem created by a more renewable, distributed, data-dependent, and demand-responsive electricity system.

## 3. Energy-System Transition

Kenya's electricity system is moving from a simpler historical model toward a more complex operating environment.

The older model was shaped around:

- Centralized generation.
- One-directional power flows.
- Passive electricity consumption.
- Limited customer-side intelligence.
- Manual or weakly automated local coordination.
- Institutional data held in separate systems.
- Infrastructure planning based mainly on historical demand and scheduled investments.

The emerging model includes:

- More variable renewable generation.
- Distributed solar, batteries, mini-grids, backup systems, and flexible loads.
- Electric mobility and charging infrastructure.
- Commercial and industrial demand growth.
- Digital meters and facility-level energy systems.
- New market, tariff, and open-access possibilities.
- Stronger need for forecasting, dispatch, visibility, cybersecurity, and data governance.
- Greater dependence on coordination between public, private, community, and facility-level actors.

This transition does not remove the need for grid reinforcement. It increases the importance of pairing physical infrastructure with software-enabled coordination, operational intelligence, and interoperable data systems.

## 4. Renewable Integration Challenge

Kenya's renewable electricity strength is a national advantage, but it also changes the system-management problem.

Geothermal can provide stable renewable baseload. Hydro can support flexibility but is affected by hydrology and climate variability. Wind and solar introduce variability that must be forecast, balanced, stored, curtailed, shifted, or coordinated with flexible demand.

As variable renewable energy grows, the electricity system requires stronger capabilities for:

- Forecasting renewable generation and demand.
- Coordinating storage and flexible loads.
- Managing congestion and local constraints.
- Procuring ancillary services.
- Supporting open access without weakening system security.
- Understanding where distributed assets exist and how they behave.
- Aligning generation, network capacity, demand, and customer-side resources.

The challenge is not that renewables are a problem. The challenge is that renewable-heavy systems require a more intelligent operating model.

## 5. Digital Energy Coordination Gap

Kenya's electricity system is becoming more digital, but the emerging digital layer remains fragmented.

Utilities, regulators, public agencies, generation companies, transmission actors, distribution operators, counties, private developers, commercial facilities, mini-grid operators, solar firms, battery providers, e-mobility companies, and consumers all generate or require energy information. However, the data and control systems needed to coordinate these actors are often separated by institutional boundaries, technical formats, commercial incentives, procurement histories, and cybersecurity constraints.

This creates a digital energy coordination gap:

> The electricity system increasingly depends on distributed, real-time, multi-actor coordination, but the digital infrastructure for observing, integrating, governing, and optimizing that coordination is still immature.

Aurex Digital Solutions is being established to study and address this gap through open research, interoperable software development, pilots, standards work, institutional partnerships, and careful validation.

## 6. Visibility Problem

A modern energy system cannot coordinate what it cannot see.

Kenya's grid, facilities, mini-grids, solar installations, batteries, backup generators, EV chargers, and flexible loads may exist across many owners and technical environments. Without sufficient visibility, system actors struggle to answer operationally important questions:

- Where are distributed energy resources located?
- Which assets are available, offline, constrained, or underused?
- Which feeders, facilities, or local networks face abnormal losses or reliability risks?
- What demand patterns are emerging at industrial, commercial, public-service, and household levels?
- Which loads can shift safely without harming users?
- Which assets can provide local flexibility or resilience?
- Which interventions reduce cost, outages, or losses most effectively?

The visibility problem is not only a data-collection problem. It is also a data-quality, permissions, interoperability, cybersecurity, governance, and decision-support problem.

## 7. Interoperability Problem

Energy systems are increasingly composed of assets and software from many vendors and institutions.

Meters, inverters, batteries, building systems, mini-grid controllers, utility systems, billing platforms, customer portals, outage systems, GIS tools, SCADA systems, forecasting tools, and analytics platforms may not communicate cleanly with one another. Even where data exists, it may be difficult to exchange, validate, interpret, or use for coordinated action.

The interoperability problem has several layers:

- Technical interoperability between devices, data formats, and APIs.
- Semantic interoperability so different actors mean the same thing when they exchange data.
- Operational interoperability between utility, facility, market, and regulatory processes.
- Governance interoperability across permissions, ownership, consent, and accountability.
- Security interoperability so integration does not create unacceptable cyber risk.

Without interoperability, digitalization can create isolated systems rather than coordinated infrastructure.

## 8. Facility-Level Problem

Facilities are where much of the energy transition becomes visible in practice.

Hospitals, schools, universities, factories, farms, cold rooms, malls, data centers, offices, charging hubs, public buildings, and apartment complexes experience energy as cost, uptime, equipment risk, productivity, comfort, safety, and service continuity.

Many facilities face a common problem:

> They consume energy in increasingly complex ways, but often lack practical tools to understand, control, forecast, optimize, and report their energy behavior.

Facility-level challenges include:

- Rising or unpredictable electricity costs.
- Limited visibility into equipment-level consumption.
- Poor integration between grid supply, solar, batteries, and backup generation.
- Inefficient peak demand and tariff exposure.
- Weak outage preparedness.
- Manual energy reporting.
- Limited demand-side participation in future flexibility or demand-response models.
- Difficulty proving savings or resilience improvements to financiers and decision-makers.

For Aurex, the facility level is an important starting point because it is practical, measurable, and close to the user. It can produce evidence before larger regional or national coordination claims are made.

## 9. Reliability Problem

Reliable electricity is a foundation for economic productivity and public welfare.

Kenya Power has reported improvements in reliability indicators, including SAIDI and SAIFI, for the year ended June 2025. However, the remaining interruption levels still indicate a material reliability challenge for customers who depend on continuous power for production, digital services, healthcare, refrigeration, education, safety, and daily life.

Reliability problems are not caused by a single actor or a single failure mode. They may emerge from:

- Network constraints.
- Equipment failure.
- Weather and environmental conditions.
- Demand growth.
- Protection and control limitations.
- Maintenance backlogs.
- Theft, vandalism, or illegal connections.
- Insufficient operational visibility.
- Slow fault detection and restoration.
- Coordination gaps between planning, operations, and customer-side systems.

Software cannot replace physical maintenance, reinforcement, and protection engineering. But software-enabled visibility, forecasting, outage intelligence, asset monitoring, and facility-level resilience planning can help reduce the operational blind spots that make reliability problems harder to manage.

## 10. Network-Loss Problem

Network losses remain one of Kenya's clearest electricity-system efficiency problems.

The IEA Kenya 2024 review estimated electricity-network losses at about 23% in 2023. Kenya Power later reported that total system losses improved from 23.16% to 21.21% in the year ended June 2025. This improvement matters, but the remaining level is still significant.

Losses affect:

- Utility financial performance.
- Customer tariffs and affordability.
- Investment capacity.
- Technical efficiency.
- Trust in the electricity system.
- The amount of generation required to serve useful demand.

Losses can be technical or commercial. Technical losses may require infrastructure upgrades, transformer improvements, network reconfiguration, voltage management, and better planning. Commercial losses may involve metering accuracy, billing, theft reduction, customer data quality, inspection workflows, and accountability systems.

Aurex should not frame digital technology as a complete solution to losses. The defensible framing is narrower and stronger:

> Better data, metering intelligence, feeder analytics, asset visibility, and operational coordination can help identify, prioritize, monitor, and evaluate loss-reduction interventions, while physical upgrades and institutional enforcement remain essential.

## 11. Affordability Problem

Electricity affordability remains a major constraint even where electricity access has improved.

High power costs affect households, small businesses, factories, public institutions, farms, digital services, and investors. Affordability is influenced by multiple factors, including generation costs, power-purchase arrangements, network losses, investment needs, foreign exchange exposure, fuel-price effects, taxes, tariffs, demand patterns, operational efficiency, and the cost of capital.

The affordability problem is therefore not reducible to one institution or one tariff line.

Aurex's role should be to investigate how software-enabled coordination can contribute to affordability by:

- Reducing avoidable waste at facility level.
- Improving demand visibility.
- Supporting tariff-aware energy management.
- Helping identify technical and commercial inefficiencies.
- Enabling future flexibility services where regulation permits.
- Supporting better measurement of savings, reliability, and productive use.
- Giving decision-makers clearer evidence for energy investments.

Digital coordination can support affordability, but it cannot substitute for tariff reform, financing reform, physical infrastructure investment, regulatory clarity, and broader sector governance.

## 12. Flexibility Problem

Flexibility is the ability of the electricity system to adjust supply, demand, storage, and network operation over time and location.

As Kenya integrates more variable renewable energy and grows demand from industry, households, public services, and electric mobility, flexibility becomes more important. Flexibility can come from generation, hydropower operations, storage, interconnectors, demand response, industrial load shifting, smart charging, batteries, and facility-level control.

The flexibility problem is that many flexible resources may exist or emerge before the system has mature ways to see them, value them, coordinate them, or govern their participation.

Key questions include:

- Which loads can shift without harming users?
- Which batteries or backup systems can support resilience or grid services?
- How should EV charging be coordinated?
- Where can distributed solar reduce local constraints, and where can it create new ones?
- What rules are needed before distributed assets can provide services to the grid?
- How can flexibility be measured, verified, compensated, and secured?

Aurex must treat flexibility as an evidence problem before treating it as a market or product claim.

## 13. Institutional Coordination Problem

Energy-system modernization requires many institutions to move in compatible directions.

Kenya's energy sector includes ministries, regulators, generation companies, transmission institutions, distribution utilities, counties, independent power producers, private developers, financiers, development partners, universities, communities, and consumers. New sectors such as e-mobility, digital infrastructure, agriculture, buildings, health, water, and industrial policy also increasingly depend on electricity planning.

The institutional coordination problem is:

> Energy decisions are increasingly interdependent, but the data, incentives, planning processes, digital tools, and implementation timelines of relevant institutions are not always aligned.

This is not a blame statement. It is a structural feature of modernization.

Aurex should position itself as a partner and steward of open coordination infrastructure, not as a substitute for existing institutions.

## 14. Cybersecurity Problem

Digital energy infrastructure creates value only if it is trustworthy.

As electricity systems become more connected, the attack surface expands. Devices, APIs, dashboards, meters, facility controllers, cloud platforms, communication networks, user accounts, operational data, and control signals can become points of vulnerability.

Cybersecurity must therefore be treated as a founding requirement, not an afterthought.

The cybersecurity problem includes:

- Protecting operational systems from unauthorized access.
- Separating research, analytics, and control environments appropriately.
- Securing device communication and identity.
- Managing software supply-chain risk.
- Defining who can access what data and for what purpose.
- Preventing open-source transparency from becoming operational exposure.
- Maintaining auditability, resilience, and recovery processes.

Open digital infrastructure in energy must be governed. Aurex should distinguish between open research, open standards, open tools, controlled pilots, regulated deployments, and safety-critical operational systems.

## 15. Legacy-Infrastructure Modernization Framing

Kenya's electricity system, like many systems globally, contains infrastructure, processes, and institutional arrangements built across different periods of technological maturity.

Legacy infrastructure should not be dismissed. It represents decades of investment and operational learning. The modernization challenge is to make existing and future infrastructure more observable, interoperable, resilient, efficient, and responsive.

The correct framing is:

> Kenya does not need software instead of grid reinforcement. Kenya needs grid reinforcement, generation planning, regulatory development, institutional coordination, and a digital operating layer that helps these investments perform better.

This framing protects Aurex from overstating software and keeps the institution focused on practical contribution.

## 16. Structural Problem Synthesis

Across the preceding categories, a common structural pattern appears:

- Renewable generation is growing, but variability requires stronger coordination.
- Access is improving, but affordability and quality of supply remain material concerns.
- Demand is growing, but demand-side visibility is still limited.
- Distributed assets are emerging, but their location, behavior, and flexibility are not fully coordinated.
- Facilities carry real energy costs and outage risks, but many lack practical energy intelligence.
- Network losses remain high, but loss-reduction work needs better measurement and targeting.
- Institutions have ambitious plans, but implementation requires coordinated data, tools, standards, and feedback loops.
- Digital tools exist in fragments, but interoperability and cybersecurity remain underdeveloped.

The structural problem is not only technical, financial, or regulatory. It sits at the intersection of infrastructure, software, institutions, markets, facilities, and public-interest governance.

## 17. Root Problem

The root problem is:

> Kenya's electricity system is becoming more renewable, distributed, data-dependent, and demand-sensitive, but its coordination architecture has not yet matured enough to make all relevant assets, facilities, institutions, and users visible, interoperable, flexible, secure, and economically optimized.

This root problem explains why Aurex Digital Solutions should begin with research, prototypes, facility-level validation, open tools, standards, and institutional partnerships rather than immediate claims of national-scale control.

## 18. Official Problem Statement

Kenya's electricity system is entering a new phase in which renewable generation, distributed energy resources, facility-level energy systems, electric mobility, growing demand, network constraints, affordability pressure, reliability expectations, and institutional modernization must be coordinated with greater precision than legacy operating models can easily support.

The central problem is not simply a shortage of electricity generation, nor is it the failure of any single institution. Kenya requires continued investment in generation, transmission, distribution, maintenance, regulation, finance, and human capacity. However, these physical and institutional investments increasingly need a complementary digital coordination layer: one that can improve visibility, interoperability, forecasting, facility-level control, demand-side intelligence, flexibility management, loss analytics, cybersecurity, and evidence-based decision-making.

Aurex Digital Solutions is being established to research, develop, validate, and steward open digital energy coordination infrastructure that can help Kenya, and later other African energy systems, translate renewable-energy potential and infrastructure investment into reliable, resilient, affordable, transparent, and productive energy service.

## 19. One-Sentence Version

Kenya's electricity system is becoming more renewable, distributed, and demand-sensitive, but it lacks a mature open digital coordination layer to make energy assets, facilities, data, institutions, and users sufficiently visible, interoperable, flexible, secure, and economically optimized.

## 20. Investor Version

Kenya has strong renewable-electricity foundations and growing electricity demand, but persistent reliability, affordability, network-loss, facility-efficiency, data, and coordination challenges create a clear infrastructure-software opportunity. Aurex Digital Solutions is being established to build and validate open digital energy coordination tools, beginning at facility level and expanding toward grid-edge and distributed-resource coordination as evidence, partnerships, regulation, and cybersecurity maturity allow.

## 21. Government Version

Kenya's energy-transition goals require not only new generation and grid investment, but also stronger coordination across data, facilities, distributed assets, utilities, regulators, counties, private developers, consumers, and emerging sectors such as e-mobility and industry. Aurex Digital Solutions proposes to support public-interest modernization by developing open, interoperable, secure, and evidence-based digital coordination infrastructure that can complement existing institutions and improve the effectiveness of energy investments.

## 22. Engineering Version

Kenya's electricity system is moving toward a higher-complexity operating environment with variable renewable generation, distributed energy resources, flexible loads, facility-level systems, electric mobility, and growing demand. The engineering problem is the absence of a mature, secure, interoperable coordination stack for telemetry, data quality, asset modeling, forecasting, control boundaries, demand-side optimization, flexibility measurement, loss analytics, and integration with existing utility and facility systems.

## 23. Problem Architecture

The problem can be understood across six layers:

1. **Physical layer**  
   Generation, transmission, distribution, substations, feeders, transformers, meters, buildings, solar systems, batteries, EV chargers, backup systems, and appliances.

2. **Data layer**  
   Meter data, asset data, outage data, tariff data, weather data, network data, customer data, facility data, generation forecasts, demand forecasts, and maintenance records.

3. **Coordination layer**  
   Dispatch, demand response, facility optimization, storage operation, smart charging, outage response, feeder prioritization, flexibility orchestration, and operational planning.

4. **Institutional layer**  
   Ministries, regulators, utilities, generators, transmission actors, counties, private developers, financiers, universities, standards bodies, and consumers.

5. **Governance and security layer**  
   Cybersecurity, privacy, data ownership, permissions, auditability, safety boundaries, regulatory compliance, procurement transparency, and public-interest safeguards.

6. **Economic layer**  
   Tariffs, losses, operating costs, capital investment, financing risk, productivity, service quality, resilience value, and affordability outcomes.

Aurex Digital Solutions should focus on the coordination, data, interoperability, facility, and governance layers while clearly acknowledging that physical and regulatory layers remain essential.

## 24. Validation Questions - What Aurex Must Prove

Aurex must treat its founding thesis as a hypothesis requiring evidence.

The institution must prove:

- Can facility-level energy management produce measurable cost, reliability, efficiency, or resilience benefits in Kenyan conditions?
- Which facility types have the strongest early need: hospitals, factories, universities, farms, cold chains, data centers, public buildings, charging hubs, or commercial sites?
- What minimum telemetry is required to make useful energy decisions without excessive cost?
- Can open-source tools reduce adoption barriers while maintaining cybersecurity and operational discipline?
- Which data standards and interoperability patterns are realistic for Kenyan energy actors?
- Can digital tools help identify and prioritize loss-reduction interventions?
- Which distributed resources can provide flexibility, and under what regulatory and technical conditions?
- How should energy data permissions, privacy, and governance be structured?
- What institutional partnerships are required for pilots to be credible?
- What outcomes should be measured: cost savings, avoided outages, demand reduction, peak shifting, loss identification, asset utilization, carbon reporting, or user satisfaction?
- Where does software create real value, and where is physical infrastructure investment the binding constraint?
- What should remain open research, what should become open tooling, and what should require controlled deployment?

## 25. Problem Boundary

Aurex Digital Solutions should be clear about what problem it is and is not claiming to solve.

### Inside The Problem Boundary

- Facility-level energy visibility and optimization.
- Open digital tools for energy monitoring, modeling, and coordination.
- Interoperability frameworks for energy data and devices.
- Distributed-energy visibility and future coordination research.
- Demand-side intelligence and flexibility validation.
- Loss analytics and decision-support tools.
- Energy data governance and cybersecurity design.
- Research, prototypes, pilots, standards, and institutional learning.

### Outside The Problem Boundary

- Acting as a national utility, regulator, or system operator.
- Claiming government approval before it exists.
- Replacing physical grid reinforcement, maintenance, or generation investment.
- Treating DERMS as already proven for Kenya before validation.
- Blaming a single institution for complex sector-wide challenges.
- Making operational control claims without regulatory authority, cybersecurity readiness, and technical validation.
- Presenting open-source software as automatically safe for safety-critical systems.

## 26. Institutional Problem-Mission Link

The problem defined in this document leads directly to the mission of Aurex Digital Solutions.

If Kenya's energy transition requires better visibility, interoperability, facility intelligence, flexibility, loss analytics, reliability support, affordability insight, cybersecurity, and institutional coordination, then Aurex Digital Solutions should exist to build the open digital infrastructure needed to support those capabilities.

The mission is therefore:

> To research, develop, validate, and steward open, interoperable, secure, intelligence-driven digital energy coordination infrastructure that helps Kenya and other African energy systems become more reliable, resilient, affordable, transparent, flexible, and economically productive.
