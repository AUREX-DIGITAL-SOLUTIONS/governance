# Task 7 - Governance Framework

## Aurex Digital Solutions

**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** Draft v1.0  
**Institution:** Aurex Digital Solutions  
**Phase:** Founding / Pre-formalization  
**Purpose:** Define how authority, accountability, decisions, oversight, and escalation operate inside Aurex

**Navigation:** [Previous: Organization Charter](./ORGANIZATION-CHARTER.md) | [Home: Documentation Index](./README.md) | [Next: Organization Structure & Decision Rights](./ORGANIZATION-STRUCTURE-AND-DECISION-RIGHTS.md)

Task 6 established what Aurex is constitutionally.

Task 7 answers:

> Who decides what, under which authority, with what accountability, and how are disputes or exceptional decisions handled?

This is the point where Aurex begins moving from informal founder-led governance toward documented governance.

---

## 1. Governance Objective

Aurex requires governance that is:

- clear;
- lightweight at the founding stage;
- scalable;
- evidence-driven;
- technically competent;
- resistant to founder dependency;
- safe for critical-infrastructure work;
- transparent enough to earn institutional trust.

The governance model should avoid two extremes:

```text
TOO LITTLE GOVERNANCE
        |
        v
founder decides everything
unclear authority
inconsistent decisions
institutional risk
```

and:

```text
TOO MUCH GOVERNANCE
        |
        v
committees everywhere
slow execution
documentation bureaucracy
no real building
```

Aurex needs minimum viable governance that can mature with the institution.

---

## 2. Foundational Governance Principle

The core rule is:

> Authority must be explicit, limited to defined scope, and matched with accountability.

Therefore:

```text
AUTHORITY
    +
RESPONSIBILITY
    +
EVIDENCE
    +
ACCOUNTABILITY
    =
LEGITIMATE DECISION
```

Authority without accountability is unacceptable.

Responsibility without authority is ineffective.

Technical capability does not create permission.

---

## 3. Governance Model

Aurex should use a layered governance model.

```text
                     GOVERNING FUNCTION
                           |
                 institutional oversight
                           |
                           v
                  EXECUTIVE FUNCTION
                           |
                       execution
                           |
            +--------------+--------------+
            |              |              |
            v              v              v
     TECHNICAL       PROGRAMME        ASSURANCE
     GOVERNANCE      GOVERNANCE       FUNCTIONS
            |              |              |
            +--------------+--------------+
                           |
                           v
                    DELIVERY TEAMS
```

At the founding stage, one person may temporarily hold several roles.

The roles themselves should still be defined separately.

That prevents:

> "The founder decided it."

from becoming the permanent governance model.

This framework defines governance functions. It does not pretend that Aurex already has a board, executive team, committee structure, legal authority, or external institutional powers that have not yet been created.

---

## 4. Governance Bodies

Aurex should plan for five primary governance functions:

1. governing function;
2. founder authority;
3. executive function;
4. technical governance;
5. programme governance and assurance.

### 4.1 Governing Function

The future form may be a board, council, member body, advisory body, or equivalent legal governing body depending on the structure Aurex later adopts.

Its role would be institutional oversight.

Responsibilities may eventually include:

- approving institutional strategy;
- approving major policies;
- appointing or overseeing executive leadership;
- approving major financial commitments;
- overseeing institutional risk;
- approving major partnerships;
- protecting mission and public-interest obligations;
- monitoring executive accountability.

At the current stage, this body may not yet formally exist.

Aurex should not invent one merely for appearance.

### 4.2 Executive Function

The executive function translates approved direction into action.

At the founding stage, this may be carried by the founder or by a small number of defined roles.

### 4.3 Technical Governance Function

The technical governance function protects architecture, quality, security, interoperability, safety, and evidence integrity.

### 4.4 Programme Governance Function

The programme governance function ensures that significant programmes have purpose, ownership, scope, evidence, milestones, risk controls, and decision gates.

### 4.5 Assurance Function

The assurance function provides review and challenge for safety, cybersecurity, legal, financial, research, public-interest, and readiness risks.

---

## 5. Founder Authority

During formation, the founder requires legitimate transitional authority.

The founder may currently exercise authority over:

- institutional direction;
- initial organizational design;
- programme initiation;
- foundational documentation;
- initial partnerships;
- initial resource allocation;
- technical direction;
- public representation.

However, founder authority is transitional, not unlimited.

The rule is:

> The founder may initiate and steward Aurex, but authority should progressively transfer into defined institutional roles and governing processes.

Founder status does not eliminate accountability.

Founder decisions should be documented where they are material, strategic, financial, technical, or externally binding.

---

## 6. Founder Reserved Decisions

During the founding stage, certain decisions may remain reserved to the founder.

Examples include:

- change to Aurex's purpose;
- change to institutional mission;
- change to foundational principles;
- dissolution proposal;
- transfer of core intellectual property;
- admission of controlling ownership;
- major change in institutional identity;
- approval of the first formal legal structure;
- amendment of foundational documents before a formal governing authority exists.

These reserved powers should be reviewed when formal governance is established.

The existence of reserved founder decisions does not create authority over external institutions, government agencies, regulators, utilities, partners, customers, or infrastructure owners.

---

## 7. Executive Leadership

Executive leadership is responsible for translating governance decisions into action.

Potential future roles could include:

- Executive Director or CEO;
- Chief Technology Officer;
- Programme Director;
- Operations Lead;
- Finance Lead;
- Research Lead.

Aurex should not create titles merely to appear established.

Roles should be created when the workload and accountability justify them.

Executive leadership should own:

```text
STRATEGY EXECUTION
PROGRAMME DELIVERY
PEOPLE
BUDGETS
OPERATIONS
PARTNERSHIPS
RISK IMPLEMENTATION
REPORTING
```

Until formal executive leadership exists, the executive function remains transitional and founder-led.

---

## 8. Technical Governance

Because Aurex intends to build digital energy infrastructure, technical authority requires its own governance.

Technical governance should oversee:

- architecture;
- engineering standards;
- interoperability;
- cybersecurity;
- technical debt;
- software quality;
- data models;
- technical specifications;
- technology selection;
- deployment readiness.

A future body could be called a Technical Steering Committee.

At Draft v1.0, this framework defines the function without pretending a committee already exists.

---

## 9. Technical Decision Rule

Significant technical decisions should follow:

```text
PROBLEM
   |
   v
REQUIREMENTS
   |
   v
OPTIONS
   |
   v
EVIDENCE
   |
   v
ARCHITECTURE DECISION
   |
   v
APPROVAL
   |
   v
IMPLEMENTATION
   |
   v
REVIEW
```

Important architecture decisions should eventually be recorded through Architecture Decision Records.

Technical claims should identify the evidence that supports them.

Technical authority should remain limited by law, safety, ownership, contracts, regulatory requirements, cybersecurity controls, and delegated institutional authority.

---

## 10. Programme Governance

Every significant Aurex programme should have:

- a defined purpose;
- programme owner;
- scope;
- budget or resource model;
- milestones;
- risk profile;
- maturity status;
- evidence requirements;
- decision gates;
- reporting mechanism;
- closure criteria.

A programme cannot simply exist because someone created a repository, presentation, concept note, or name.

Programme creation should be deliberate, documented, and aligned with the Charter.

---

## 11. Programme Owner

Every programme should have one accountable owner.

Not:

```text
EVERYONE IS RESPONSIBLE
```

because that often means:

```text
NO ONE IS RESPONSIBLE
```

A programme owner is accountable for:

- objectives;
- delivery;
- reporting;
- risk;
- resource requests;
- escalation;
- evidence;
- programme records.

At the current stage, the founder may temporarily act as programme owner where no separate role has been assigned.

As soon as a programme involves meaningful contributors, funding, external commitments, pilots, or infrastructure risk, ownership should be explicit.

---

## 12. Assurance Functions

Aurex should distinguish execution from assurance.

The people building something should not always be the only people determining whether it is safe or ready.

Assurance may eventually include:

- cybersecurity review;
- safety review;
- financial review;
- legal review;
- research integrity;
- public-interest review;
- regulatory readiness.

This creates:

```text
BUILD
   |
   v
REVIEW
   |
   v
CHALLENGE
   |
   v
APPROVE / REJECT / CONDITIONALLY APPROVE
```

At the founding stage, assurance may be lightweight and evidence-based rather than committee-based.

For high-risk work, Aurex should seek qualified review before claiming readiness or deployment maturity.

---

## 13. Decision Classes

Not all decisions require the same governance.

Aurex should use four decision classes.

### Class 1 - Operational

Routine reversible decisions.

Examples:

- documentation changes;
- routine engineering work;
- scheduling;
- low-risk tooling choices.

Authority: delegated role or team.

### Class 2 - Material

Decisions with meaningful resource, architectural, legal, safety, reputational, or institutional impact.

Examples:

- major software dependency;
- pilot design;
- significant expenditure;
- external collaboration;
- public technical claim.

Authority: accountable executive, founder, or programme role within delegated limits.

### Class 3 - Strategic

Decisions affecting institutional direction.

Examples:

- new flagship programme;
- major partnership;
- funding model;
- entry into a new country;
- major organizational restructuring;
- public commitment to deployment or operations.

Authority: founder or future executive leadership with governing oversight when such oversight exists.

### Class 4 - Reserved / Constitutional

Decisions affecting the foundational identity, authority, ownership, or existence of Aurex.

Examples:

- mission change;
- Charter amendment;
- transfer of core institutional control;
- dissolution;
- merger;
- fundamental governance changes.

Authority: highest authorized governance level. During the founding period, this may remain reserved to the founder until formal governance is created.

---

## 14. Decision Rights

Every significant decision should identify:

```text
WHO PROPOSES?
WHO REVIEWS?
WHO DECIDES?
WHO EXECUTES?
WHO MUST BE INFORMED?
```

This is more useful than an organization chart alone.

[Task 8 - Organization Structure & Decision Rights](./ORGANIZATION-STRUCTURE-AND-DECISION-RIGHTS.md) formalizes these rights in greater detail.

Decision rights should be attached to roles, not personalities, wherever possible.

---

## 15. RACI Is Not Enough

RACI can help operational clarity:

- Responsible;
- Accountable;
- Consulted;
- Informed.

But RACI does not define legal or institutional authority.

Therefore Aurex should treat:

> RACI as workflow clarity, not as a substitute for decision rights.

[Task 8 - Organization Structure & Decision Rights](./ORGANIZATION-STRUCTURE-AND-DECISION-RIGHTS.md) uses decision-rights roles to identify actual decision authority.

---

## 16. Delegation

Authority should be delegable.

A valid delegation should specify:

- delegating authority;
- recipient;
- scope;
- decision limits;
- financial limits;
- duration;
- reporting requirements;
- revocation mechanism.

Example:

```text
Founder
   |
   v delegates
Programme Lead
   |
   v
May approve pilot expenses
up to defined limit
for defined programme
during defined period
```

Delegation should never be assumed.

Delegated authority cannot exceed the authority of the delegating person or body.

---

## 17. Escalation

People need to know when a decision should move upward.

Escalation triggers may include:

- safety risk;
- cybersecurity incident;
- material budget deviation;
- regulatory uncertainty;
- major programme delay;
- conflict of interest;
- reputational risk;
- significant technical failure;
- unresolved authority dispute;
- legal uncertainty;
- external stakeholder dispute.

Escalation should be encouraged where risk exceeds delegated authority.

Escalation is not disloyalty. It is part of responsible governance.

---

## 18. Emergency Authority

Critical-infrastructure work may require rapid decisions.

Emergency authority should allow designated roles to take immediate action to protect:

- people;
- equipment;
- data;
- systems;
- essential operations.

But:

> Emergency authority must not become a permanent bypass of governance.

Emergency actions should be documented and reviewed afterward.

Emergency authority does not authorize unlawful action or unauthorized control over external infrastructure.

---

## 19. Stop-Work Authority

Stop-work authority should be explicit.

Anyone involved in technical, field, security, data, or infrastructure-related work should be able to raise a stop-work concern where there is a credible risk to:

- human safety;
- electrical safety;
- equipment;
- cybersecurity;
- critical infrastructure;
- unlawful data access;
- unauthorized operational control.

This does not mean every disagreement stops a project.

It means safety, legal, and security concerns receive immediate review.

No one should be penalized for raising a good-faith stop-work concern.

---

## 20. Conflict of Interest Governance

Anyone involved in a decision should disclose a material conflict.

A conflicted person may need to:

- disclose;
- abstain;
- recuse;
- submit to independent review.

Example:

A person evaluating a vendor should disclose if they have a financial interest in that vendor.

Conflict management should be documented for material decisions.

Undisclosed conflicts can compromise trust even where the decision itself is technically sound.

---

## 21. Governance of Partnerships

Major partnerships should have:

- documented objective;
- scope;
- responsible owner;
- legal basis;
- financial terms where applicable;
- data terms;
- intellectual-property terms;
- authority boundaries;
- exit provisions;
- public-communication rules.

A meeting, discussion, letter, or memorandum should never be represented as more than it legally is.

Partnership language must distinguish:

```text
DISCUSSION
   !=
ENDORSEMENT
   !=
FORMAL PARTNERSHIP
   !=
LEGAL AUTHORITY
   !=
OPERATIONAL PERMISSION
```

---

## 22. Governance of Funding

Funding should not buy technical conclusions.

Aurex should maintain:

> Funding supports research and execution; it does not predetermine evidence.

Material funder influence should be disclosed where relevant.

Funding decisions should identify:

- source;
- purpose;
- restrictions;
- reporting obligations;
- intellectual-property implications;
- conflict risks;
- public-interest risks;
- termination conditions.

Task 16 will define the detailed Funding & Financial Framework.

---

## 23. Governance of Research

Research governance should ensure:

- clear hypothesis;
- documented methodology;
- evidence preservation;
- reproducibility where appropriate;
- conflict disclosure;
- negative-result reporting;
- ethical review where applicable;
- accurate communication of maturity and limitations.

Research findings should not be edited merely to satisfy a sponsor, partner, funder, or internal narrative.

Research claims should distinguish between:

```text
HYPOTHESIS
   |
   v
EXPERIMENT
   |
   v
EVIDENCE
   |
   v
VALIDATED FINDING
   |
   v
DEPLOYMENT CLAIM
```

---

## 24. Governance of AI

AI systems may advise, analyze, optimize, forecast, summarize, or assist.

But governance must specify:

```text
WHAT AI MAY RECOMMEND
WHAT AI MAY AUTOMATE
WHAT REQUIRES HUMAN APPROVAL
WHO IS ACCOUNTABLE
HOW DECISIONS ARE AUDITED
```

Default rule:

> AI capability does not automatically create decision authority.

AI use in energy infrastructure contexts should consider:

- safety;
- explainability;
- bias;
- data rights;
- cybersecurity;
- auditability;
- human oversight;
- failure modes;
- regulatory constraints.

---

## 25. Governance of Data

Aurex should identify for important datasets:

- owner;
- steward;
- authorized users;
- permitted purposes;
- retention;
- classification;
- security controls;
- consent or legal basis where applicable;
- sharing rules;
- deletion or archival requirements.

No dataset should be considered institutionally ownerless.

Data governance should distinguish between:

```text
PUBLIC DATA
INTERNAL DATA
PARTNER DATA
PERSONAL DATA
INFRASTRUCTURE DATA
SECURITY-SENSITIVE DATA
```

The right to process data must not be assumed merely because Aurex has technical access to it.

---

## 26. Governance of Open Source

Open-source projects should have defined:

- maintainer authority;
- contribution rules;
- merge rights;
- release process;
- security process;
- licensing;
- technical decision process;
- issue triage;
- vulnerability handling;
- repository access controls.

Repository access should reflect institutional roles, not friendship or convenience.

Open-source governance must not expose sensitive infrastructure, credentials, private data, or security details.

Task 9 now defines the detailed [Open-Source & Licensing Framework](./OPEN-SOURCE-AND-LICENSING-FRAMEWORK.md).

## 27. Governance Transparency

Aurex should publicly disclose appropriate governance information as it matures.

Potential disclosures include:

- governance structure;
- Charter;
- Code of Conduct;
- contribution model;
- programme statuses;
- major public policies;
- public research outputs.

However, confidentiality may remain necessary for:

- personal information;
- cybersecurity;
- commercial contracts;
- sensitive infrastructure;
- legal matters;
- partner confidentiality.

Transparency should be accurate, not theatrical.

Aurex should disclose what exists and avoid implying formal maturity that has not yet been reached.

## 30. Governance Cadence

Governance needs rhythm.

A future mature model may contain:

**Weekly**

- operational reviews.

**Monthly**

- programme and risk review.

**Quarterly**

- strategic and financial review.

**Annually**

- institutional strategy, governance, and Charter review.

At the current stage, Aurex should keep cadence lightweight.

The initial cadence should prioritize documentation of significant decisions, active risks, programme status, and next actions.

---

## 31. Governance Performance

Governance itself must be reviewed.

Questions include:

- Are decisions timely?
- Are authorities clear?
- Are risks escalated?
- Are programmes accountable?
- Are decisions documented?
- Is governance creating unnecessary delay?
- Is the founder still an institutional bottleneck?
- Are external claims accurate?
- Are technical decisions evidence-based?

Good governance is not measured by the number of meetings.

It is measured by whether authority is clear, decisions are traceable, risk is handled, and the institution can mature beyond informal discretion.

## 32. Founder-to-Institution Transition

This is one of the most important governance paths.

```text
STAGE 1
Founder-led
        |
        v
STAGE 2
Founder + defined roles
        |
        v
STAGE 3
Executive functions
        |
        v
STAGE 4
Independent oversight
        |
        v
STAGE 5
Institutional governance
```

The founder's influence may remain significant.

But the institution should become capable of operating when the founder is absent.

The transition should be deliberate, documented, and paced to real maturity rather than performed for appearance.

---

## 33. Governance Maturity Model

Aurex should use five governance maturity stages.

### G0 - Informal

Decisions are mostly personal.

Evidence, authority, and records may be inconsistent.

### G1 - Documented

Roles and basic procedures exist.

Major decisions begin to be recorded.

### G2 - Delegated

Authority is distributed through defined roles.

Delegation, escalation, and decision limits are clear.

### G3 - Assured

Independent review exists for significant risks.

Technical, safety, security, financial, legal, and research claims receive appropriate challenge.

### G4 - Institutional

Governance is repeatable, accountable, and resilient beyond individuals.

Authority rests in defined institutional roles and governing processes.

Aurex is presently moving from G0 toward G1.

That is appropriate for the current stage.

## 35. Interim Governance Rules

Until formal governance bodies exist:

1. The founder holds transitional institutional authority.
2. Significant decisions should be documented.
3. Technical claims require appropriate evidence.
4. Safety and legal constraints override founder preference.
5. Conflicts of interest should be disclosed.
6. Programme owners should be identified once programmes become active.
7. Material external commitments should be recorded.
8. Repository and system permissions should follow roles.
9. High-risk actions require additional review where practical.
10. The governance framework must evolve as participation increases.

These rules are interim controls, not a final operating model.

## 36. Governance Boundaries

Aurex governance governs Aurex.

It cannot govern external actors simply because they interact with Aurex technology.

For example:

```text
AUREX GOVERNANCE
      |
      v
Aurex people
Aurex programmes
Aurex systems
Aurex resources

NOT automatically

Kenya Power
EPRA
KETRACO
government agencies
customers
partners
asset owners
market operators
communities
```

External relationships depend on contracts, regulation, lawful authority, ownership, delegation, licenses, or other recognized permissions.
