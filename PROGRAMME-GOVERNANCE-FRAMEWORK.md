# Task 19 - Programme Governance Framework
## Aurex Digital Solutions

**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** Foundational Draft / Draft v1.0  
**Institution:** Aurex Digital Solutions  
**Phase:** External / Institutional Development  
**Scope:** Programme initiation, governance, ownership, decision rights, resources, risk, assurance, delivery, transition and closure  
**Navigation:** [Previous: Research & Innovation Framework](./RESEARCH-AND-INNOVATION-FRAMEWORK.md) | [Home: Documentation Index](./README.md) | Next task: Task 20 - Legal & Institutional Readiness

Task 18 established how Aurex turns uncertainty into evidence and validated technical understanding.

Task 19 establishes how Aurex converts a **validated problem, credible evidence and an emerging capability into a governed programme with explicit objectives, ownership, authority and accountability.**

> **A programme exists to produce defined outcomes—not merely to keep projects active.**

---

## 1. Purpose

The Programme Governance Framework defines how Aurex should:

- initiate programmes;
- decide whether a programme should exist;
- define programme objectives;
- establish programme ownership;
- assign authority;
- organize projects under a programme;
- govern funding and resources;
- manage risk;
- integrate research and engineering;
- control technical maturity;
- govern pilots;
- manage deployment transitions;
- review programme performance;
- suspend or terminate programmes;
- transition successful capabilities into operations.

Aurex should not allow ambitious ideas to become institutional programmes merely because they are technically interesting.

---

## 2. Programme Doctrine

The core flow is:

```text
VALIDATED PROBLEM
        ↓
EVIDENCE
        ↓
PROGRAMME CONCEPT
        ↓
BUSINESS / PUBLIC-INTEREST CASE
        ↓
DEFINED OUTCOMES
        ↓
GOVERNANCE
        ↓
RESOURCES
        ↓
PROJECTS / WORKSTREAMS
        ↓
EVIDENCE GATES
        ↓
OUTCOMES
        ↓
EVALUATION
        ↓
TRANSITION / SCALE / CLOSE
```

The programme exists because there is a defined outcome to achieve.

---

## 3. Programme Principles

Aurex programmes should follow these principles:

1. **Validated problem before programme**
2. **Outcome before activity**
3. **One accountable programme owner**
4. **Authority must be explicit**
5. **Funding does not create technical authority**
6. **Technical capability does not create deployment authority**
7. **Research evidence must remain challengeable**
8. **Safety before delivery pressure**
9. **Risk before uncontrolled scale**
10. **Pilot before production**
11. **Evidence before expansion**
12. **Programme maturity must be truthful**
13. **Programmes serve Aurex's mission**
14. **Programmes can be stopped**

---

## 4. Programme vs Project

A programme is not simply a large project.

### Programme

A coordinated body of work intended to produce broader outcomes or capabilities.

### Project

A bounded piece of work with specific deliverables.

Conceptually:

```text
PROGRAMME
   ├── PROJECT A
   ├── PROJECT B
   ├── RESEARCH WORKSTREAM
   ├── ENGINEERING WORKSTREAM
   └── PILOT / VALIDATION WORK
```

Projects create outputs.

Programmes coordinate those outputs toward outcomes.

---

## 5. Programme vs Function

A programme is also not a permanent department.

```text
FUNCTION
=
ongoing institutional capability

PROGRAMME
=
organized temporary or evolving effort toward defined outcomes
```

For example:

- Research & Innovation may be a function.
- Facility EMS validation may be a programme or programme workstream.
- A specific laboratory build may be a project.

---

## 6. Programme Initiation

A programme should begin only when Aurex can answer:

```text
WHAT PROBLEM IS BEING SOLVED?
WHO EXPERIENCES IT?
WHAT EVIDENCE EXISTS?
WHY A PROGRAMME?
WHAT OUTCOME IS SOUGHT?
WHY NOW?
WHAT CAPABILITY IS REQUIRED?
WHAT RESOURCES ARE REQUIRED?
WHAT AUTHORITY IS REQUIRED?
WHAT RISKS EXIST?
```

If these questions cannot yet be answered, research may be more appropriate than programme initiation.

---

## 7. Programme Entry Gate

Before establishing a material programme, Aurex should have sufficient evidence of:

- a defined problem;
- relevant stakeholders;
- strategic alignment;
- plausible technical pathway;
- public-interest or commercial value;
- resource plausibility;
- acceptable initial risk;
- a credible accountable owner.

This is not necessarily proof of final success.

It is evidence that structured programme investment is justified.

---

## 8. Programme Concept

A programme concept should establish:

- problem;
- intended beneficiaries;
- proposed outcome;
- why Aurex is relevant;
- initial scope;
- major assumptions;
- dependencies;
- potential partners;
- technical direction;
- preliminary risk;
- evidence still required.

A concept is not yet authorization to execute at scale.

---

## 9. Programme Business Case

A material programme should progressively develop a business case or equivalent public-interest case.

It should answer:

```text
PROBLEM
+
VALUE
+
OPTIONS
+
COST
+
RISK
+
CAPABILITY
+
EVIDENCE
+
AUTHORITY
=
CASE FOR ACTION
```

The structure should fit the nature of the programme.

Not every programme needs a commercial return.

---

## 10. Public-Interest Case

Some Aurex programmes may justify themselves through:

- reliability;
- resilience;
- interoperability;
- energy access;
- system visibility;
- capacity building;
- public infrastructure value.

The programme case should therefore distinguish:

```text
COMMERCIAL VALUE
PUBLIC VALUE
TECHNICAL VALUE
INSTITUTIONAL VALUE
RESEARCH VALUE
```

---

## 11. Programme Objectives

Programme objectives should be:

- specific;
- evidence-linked;
- measurable where practical;
- tied to a validated problem;
- realistic for the current maturity level.

Weak objective:

> Build a National DERMS.

Stronger objective:

> Determine whether multi-facility DER coordination can create measurable operational value under a defined regional use case.

---

## 12. Outputs, Outcomes and Impact

Aurex should distinguish:

```text
OUTPUT
   ↓
OUTCOME
   ↓
IMPACT
```

Example:

**Output:** Facility EMS integration deployed in a controlled environment.

**Outcome:** Heterogeneous resources become reliably observable and coordinated.

**Impact:** Improved operational efficiency or reliability.

A programme should not claim impact merely because an output was delivered.

---

## 13. Programme Scope

Programme scope should define:

- included objectives;
- included systems;
- included stakeholders;
- geographic or operational boundaries;
- technical boundaries;
- excluded activities.

Scope protects against uncontrolled programme expansion.

---

## 14. Out of Scope

A programme should explicitly define what it does **not** currently cover.

For example, an early Facility EMS programme might exclude:

- national dispatch;
- market participation;
- utility control;
- nationwide telemetry;
- regulatory functions.

This prevents vision from silently becoming current scope.

---

## 15. Programme Owner

Every material programme should have **one accountable programme owner**.

The owner is accountable for:

- objectives;
- programme coherence;
- decision escalation;
- resource use;
- risk;
- reporting;
- outcome evaluation.

Multiple contributors may lead workstreams.

There should still be one accountable owner.

---

## 16. Current Founder-Led Reality

At Aurex's current stage, the founder may initially act as:

- programme proposer;
- programme owner;
- technical lead;
- institutional decision-maker.

This concentration should be documented honestly.

It should not be disguised through fictional roles.

As scale and consequence increase, roles should progressively separate.

This framework does not invent staff, committees, partners, pilots, sites, budgets, contracts, approvals, government endorsements, utility endorsements, operational deployments, national authority, regulatory authority or production maturity that do not yet exist.

---

## 17. Programme Authority

Programme ownership does not create unlimited authority.

A programme owner may only act within:

- Aurex governance;
- delegated authority;
- approved scope;
- budget authority;
- contracts;
- applicable law;
- regulatory limits;
- asset-owner authority;
- safety requirements.

---

## 18. Programme Authority Boundary

Aurex should preserve:

```text
PROGRAMME AUTHORITY
        ≠
LEGAL AUTHORITY
        ≠
REGULATORY AUTHORITY
        ≠
ASSET OWNERSHIP
        ≠
SYSTEM-OPERATION AUTHORITY
```

In shorthand:

`PROGRAMME AUTHORITY ≠ LEGAL AUTHORITY ≠ REGULATORY AUTHORITY ≠ ASSET OWNERSHIP ≠ SYSTEM-OPERATION AUTHORITY`

This is especially important for energy infrastructure.

---

## 19. Programme Governance Model

A material programme should answer:

```text
WHO PROPOSES?
WHO REVIEWS?
WHO DECIDES?
WHO EXECUTES?
WHO ASSURES?
WHO IS INFORMED?
```

This connects Task 7 governance and Task 8 decision rights directly to programme execution.

---

## 20. Programme Decision Rights

The PRAEI model applies:

```text
P — PROPOSE
R — REVIEW
A — APPROVE
E — EXECUTE
I — INFORM
```

Programme-specific decision rights should be documented for material decisions.

---

## 21. Decision Classes

Programme decisions may be:

### Operational

Routine execution within approved scope.

### Material

Significant architecture, cost, schedule or risk changes.

### Strategic

Major programme direction or stakeholder implications.

### Reserved / Constitutional

Decisions affecting Aurex's mission, governance or institutional boundaries.

Decision authority should increase with consequence.

---

## 22. Programme Charter

A material programme should eventually have a programme charter.

It may contain:

- programme name;
- problem;
- purpose;
- objectives;
- outcomes;
- scope;
- owner;
- authority;
- major stakeholders;
- funding;
- key risks;
- maturity;
- evidence gates;
- reporting;
- exit conditions.

A programme charter should not be confused with Aurex's Organization Charter.

---

## 23. Programme Maturity

Aurex should use explicit programme maturity.

Suggested internal model:

```text
PG0 — CONCEPT
PG1 — RESEARCH
PG2 — VALIDATED CASE
PG3 — PROTOTYPE
PG4 — PILOT
PG5 — PROGRAMME DELIVERY
PG6 — OPERATIONAL TRANSITION
PG7 — SCALE / MATURE PROGRAMME
```

This is an internal governance model.

It should not be presented as a formal external standard.

---

## 24. Maturity Is Earned

```text
CONCEPT
  ≠
PROGRAMME

PROTOTYPE
  ≠
PILOT

PILOT
  ≠
PRODUCTION

PRODUCTION
  ≠
NATIONAL INFRASTRUCTURE
```

Each transition requires additional evidence and authority.

---

## 25. Evidence Gates

Programme advancement should occur through explicit gates.

```text
CONCEPT
   ↓
PROBLEM VALIDATED?
   ↓
RESEARCH
   ↓
TECHNICAL CASE VALIDATED?
   ↓
PROTOTYPE
   ↓
CONTROLLED PERFORMANCE?
   ↓
PILOT
   ↓
REAL-WORLD VALUE?
   ↓
PROGRAMME DELIVERY
   ↓
OPERATIONAL READINESS?
```

A failed gate can trigger redesign, delay or closure.

---

## 26. Gate Decision

At a maturity gate, Aurex should choose:

```text
PROCEED
HOLD
REVISE
PIVOT
STOP
```

Proceed should not be the default.

---

## 27. Research Integration

Task 18 remains active throughout a programme.

Programme pressure must not convert research into marketing.

Research should continue to challenge:

- assumptions;
- architecture;
- economics;
- stakeholder value;
- scalability;
- risks.

---

## 28. Research-to-Programme Boundary

A research finding may justify programme development.

It does not automatically authorize programme execution.

```text
RESEARCH FINDING
      ↓
PROGRAMME CASE
      ↓
GOVERNANCE REVIEW
      ↓
AUTHORIZED PROGRAMME
```

Research evidence remains challengeable after a programme begins.

---

## 29. Engineering Integration

Technical work within a programme should follow:

```text
PROGRAMME NEED
     ↓
VALIDATED REQUIREMENT
     ↓
ARCHITECTURE
     ↓
ENGINEERING
     ↓
TESTING
     ↓
VALIDATION
```

Engineering should not silently expand programme scope.

---

## 30. Architecture Governance

Material architecture decisions should remain documented through appropriate records such as ADRs.

The programme should not override Task 7 or Task 11 technical governance merely to accelerate delivery.

---

## 31. Security Integration

Every programme should assess:

- data sensitivity;
- cyber risk;
- access;
- control capability;
- external dependencies;
- incident response;
- security testing.

Security should be built into programme maturity gates.

---

## 32. Safety Integration

Any programme capable of influencing physical energy infrastructure must define:

- local protection boundaries;
- command authority;
- manual override where applicable;
- failure behavior;
- safe state;
- testing environment;
- emergency stop/escalation.

> **Safety before schedule.**

---

## 33. Ethics and Public Interest

Task 12 applies to all programmes.

Programme review should ask:

```text
WHO BENEFITS?
WHO BEARS RISK?
WHO CONTROLS THE SYSTEM?
WHO CONTROLS DATA?
WHAT DEPENDENCY IS CREATED?
WHAT PUBLIC-INTEREST EFFECT EXISTS?
```

---

## 34. Data Governance

Programmes should define:

- required data;
- purpose;
- access;
- ownership/control where applicable;
- retention;
- sharing;
- security;
- programme exit handling.

Data collection should not expand merely because infrastructure exists to collect more.

---

## 35. Programme Stakeholders

Task 17 applies.

Programmes should identify:

- beneficiaries;
- asset owners;
- operators;
- users;
- partners;
- regulators;
- funders;
- affected communities;
- technology providers.

Stakeholders should not all be treated as having equal authority.

---

## 36. Stakeholder Authority

Programme governance should explicitly map:

```text
WHO OWNS?
WHO OPERATES?
WHO REGULATES?
WHO FUNDS?
WHO USES?
WHO MAINTAINS?
WHO MAY AUTHORIZE CONTROL?
```

This becomes essential as programmes interact with real infrastructure.

---

## 37. Programme Funding

Task 16 applies.

Funding should be linked to:

- defined programme objectives;
- maturity;
- deliverables;
- evidence gates;
- risk.

Funding availability should not cause the programme to skip maturity stages.

---

## 38. Programme Budget

A programme budget should progressively identify:

- personnel;
- hardware;
- software;
- facilities;
- testing;
- travel;
- professional services;
- contingency;
- operations;
- assurance.

The budget should remain proportional to the current programme stage and should not pretend that resources exist before they do.

---

## 39. Programme Cost Control

Material changes should ask:

```text
WHY DID COST CHANGE?
WHAT SCOPE CHANGED?
WHAT RISK CHANGED?
WHAT OUTCOME CHANGED?
IS ADDITIONAL FUNDING JUSTIFIED?
```

Costs should not simply expand because a programme is considered strategically important.

---

## 40. Programme Risk

Task 13 applies directly.

Programme risks may include:

- technical failure;
- safety;
- cybersecurity;
- schedule;
- funding;
- stakeholder withdrawal;
- regulatory barriers;
- vendor dependency;
- data limitations;
- capability shortages;
- reputational risk.

Material risks should be registered and owned.

---

## 41. Programme Risk Owner

A programme owner is not automatically the owner of every programme risk.

Each material risk should have a specific accountable risk owner where practical.

Where Aurex has not yet created separate roles, the concentration of responsibility should be documented rather than hidden.

---

## 42. Programme Assumptions

Major programme assumptions should be explicit.

Example:

> Facility operators will provide sufficiently useful telemetry access for the proposed integration approach.

This may be critical enough to require validation before proceeding.

---

## 43. Assumption Gate

```text
ASSUMPTION
     ↓
EVIDENCE REQUIRED
     ↓
VALIDATE
     ↓
SUPPORTED / MODIFIED / REJECTED
```

A critical rejected assumption may require programme redesign.

---

## 44. Programme Dependencies

Dependencies may include:

- upstream software;
- hardware vendors;
- connectivity;
- data access;
- regulatory interpretation;
- funding;
- partnerships;
- facilities;
- specialist expertise.

Dependencies should be visible before they become failure points.

---

## 45. Programme Workstreams

A programme may contain workstreams such as:

```text
RESEARCH
ENGINEERING
SECURITY
PARTNERSHIPS
FINANCE
LEGAL
OPERATIONS
PILOT
ASSURANCE
```

These are workstreams where needed, not permanent departments.

---

## 46. Programme Projects

Projects should have:

- defined objective;
- owner;
- scope;
- deliverables;
- timeline where sufficiently known;
- risks;
- dependencies;
- completion criteria.

A programme should not become a collection of unmanaged tasks.

---

## 47. Definition of Done

Every material project or workstream should define completion before execution where practical.

Example:

> Device integration is complete when telemetry is reliable, documented, tested under defined failure conditions and reproducible in the reference environment.

---

## 48. Activity vs Progress

Programme governance should distinguish:

```text
MEETINGS
COMMITS
REPORTS
PURCHASES
      ≠
PROGRAMME PROGRESS
```

Progress means movement toward defined outcomes.

---

## 49. Pilot Governance

A pilot is a controlled real-world validation stage.

It should have:

- objective;
- site;
- authority;
- baseline;
- technical configuration;
- success criteria;
- safety plan;
- security plan;
- monitoring;
- evaluation;
- termination conditions.

---

## 50. Pilot Entry Gate

Before a pilot:

```text
PROBLEM VALIDATED?
TECHNOLOGY READY ENOUGH?
SITE AUTHORIZED?
DATA RIGHTS CLEAR?
CONTROL AUTHORITY CLEAR?
SAFETY ACCEPTABLE?
SECURITY ACCEPTABLE?
SUCCESS CRITERIA DEFINED?
SUPPORT AVAILABLE?
```

If not, the pilot should not proceed.

Real site authority, asset authority, data authority, control authority, safety, security, baseline evidence and predefined success criteria must exist before a pilot is represented as a pilot.

---

## 51. Pilot Is Not Production

A pilot may involve real infrastructure.

It still differs from production because:

- duration may be limited;
- support may be exceptional;
- scope may be narrow;
- risk controls may be temporary;
- technology may still require active research.

A successful pilot must undergo an explicit operational-readiness decision.

---

## 52. Pilot Success

Pilot success should be based on predefined criteria.

Potential categories:

- technical performance;
- operational value;
- safety;
- cybersecurity;
- economics;
- user acceptance;
- maintainability;
- stakeholder value.

The programme should report failures as well as successes.

---

## 53. Pilot Failure

A pilot may fail because:

- the hypothesis was wrong;
- architecture was unsuitable;
- integration costs were excessive;
- data was unreliable;
- stakeholder value was low;
- operations were unsafe;
- dependencies were unacceptable.

Failure should produce learning.

---

## 54. Deployment Gate

Before moving from pilot to broader deployment:

```text
VALUE VALIDATED?
RELIABILITY VALIDATED?
SECURITY VALIDATED?
OPERATIONS DEFINED?
SUPPORT DEFINED?
FUNDING SUSTAINABLE?
AUTHORITY CLEAR?
LEGAL POSITION CLEAR?
RISKS ACCEPTABLE?
```

Deployment should not simply mean repeat the pilot.

---

## 55. Transition to Operations

Programme capability becomes operational only when ongoing responsibility is established.

Required questions include:

```text
WHO OPERATES?
WHO SUPPORTS?
WHO MAINTAINS?
WHO PAYS?
WHO OWNS INCIDENTS?
WHO PATCHES?
WHO MONITORS?
WHO AUTHORIZES CHANGES?
```

If these questions have no answer, the capability is not operationally mature.

---

## 56. Operational Acceptance

Operational transition should document:

- service boundaries;
- system owner;
- operational owner;
- technical support;
- security responsibility;
- monitoring;
- maintenance;
- incident response;
- documentation;
- backup/recovery;
- change management.

This framework does not assert that Aurex currently has operational acceptance for any infrastructure deployment.

---

## 57. Programme-to-Operations Principle

> **Pilot success is not operational readiness.**

The programme must demonstrate that the capability can be sustained after exceptional project attention ends.

---

## 58. Programme Performance

Programme performance should measure:

- outcomes;
- milestone evidence;
- quality;
- risk;
- budget;
- stakeholder value;
- technical maturity;
- learning.

Vanity activity should not replace outcome measurement.

---

## 59. Programme KPIs

KPIs should be:

- decision-useful;
- linked to objectives;
- measurable;
- not easily manipulated.

Potential categories include:

- technical reliability;
- integration success;
- validation results;
- programme cost;
- risk status;
- stakeholder adoption;
- operational outcomes.

Specific KPIs should be programme-defined.

---

## 60. Programme Reporting

Programme reporting should answer:

```text
WHAT WERE WE TRYING TO ACHIEVE?
WHAT DID WE COMPLETE?
WHAT EVIDENCE EXISTS?
WHAT CHANGED?
WHAT IS BLOCKED?
WHAT RISKS CHANGED?
WHAT DECISIONS ARE REQUIRED?
WHAT IS NEXT?
```

---

## 61. Reporting Frequency

Reporting should be proportional to programme stage, risk and stakeholder consequence.

A concept may need light records.

A pilot affecting real assets may need more frequent reporting, stronger logs and clearer escalation.

---

## 62. Programme Records

Material programme records may include:

- concept note;
- business or public-interest case;
- charter;
- decision log;
- risk register;
- assumption register;
- dependency register;
- budget record;
- stakeholder map;
- evidence gate records;
- pilot plan;
- pilot evaluation;
- closure report.

Records should support decisions, not become performative paperwork.

---

## 63. System of Record

Aurex should progressively define where programme records live and which version controls institutional truth.

Until a more mature system exists, the authoritative record should remain simple, traceable and reviewable.

A private conversation, sales deck or informal message should not silently become the system of record for programme authority.

---

## 64. Change and Scope Control

Material programme changes should be explicit.

A change may affect:

- objectives;
- scope;
- technical architecture;
- budget;
- schedule;
- risk;
- stakeholders;
- authority;
- operational boundary.

Unapproved scope expansion should not be disguised as iteration.

---

## 65. Change Decision Test

Before approving a change, Aurex should ask:

```text
WHAT IS CHANGING?
WHY IS IT CHANGING?
WHAT EVIDENCE SUPPORTS THE CHANGE?
WHAT RISK CHANGES?
WHO HAS AUTHORITY?
WHAT RECORD MUST BE UPDATED?
```

This protects programme coherence.

---

## 66. Programme Assurance

Assurance asks whether the programme is being governed, executed and represented truthfully.

Assurance may review:

- evidence quality;
- decision records;
- risk controls;
- architecture decisions;
- safety controls;
- security controls;
- stakeholder claims;
- budget use;
- pilot readiness;
- operational readiness.

---

## 67. Builder vs Reviewer

As Aurex matures, the person building a programme should not always be the only person reviewing it.

At the current founder-led stage, separation may be limited.

Where separation does not yet exist, Aurex should record the limitation and avoid overstating assurance independence.

---

## 68. Quality Review

Quality review should assess whether programme outputs satisfy defined requirements and completion criteria.

Quality does not mean that every preferred idea succeeded.

It means work met the stated standard or the gap is visible.

---

## 69. Security Review

Security review should occur before pilots, integrations, external access or any system that handles sensitive operational data or control capability.

Security review should be proportionate to consequence.

A convenience argument should not override security requirements.

---

## 70. Safety Review

Safety review is required before any programme activity can affect physical energy assets or operating conditions.

It should address failure modes, manual override, local authority, escalation, emergency procedures and safe rollback.

Safety risk should be treated as a programme decision factor, not an afterthought.

---

## 71. Stop-Work Authority

Aurex should preserve the ability to pause or stop programme work when:

- safety becomes unacceptable;
- security risk becomes unacceptable;
- legal or regulatory authority is unclear;
- evidence fails a gate;
- stakeholder authority is withdrawn;
- programme claims become inaccurate;
- funding conditions compromise integrity.

Stop-work authority protects the institution and the public interest.

---

## 72. Suspension

Suspension means pausing programme work while a material issue is reviewed.

Reasons may include:

- unresolved safety issue;
- security incident;
- loss of site authority;
- unresolved legal question;
- critical dependency failure;
- budget uncertainty;
- failed evidence gate.

Suspension is not failure by itself.

---

## 73. Termination

Termination means the programme should end rather than continue consuming institutional capacity.

Termination may be appropriate when:

- the problem is not validated;
- the solution does not work;
- the value case fails;
- risks remain unacceptable;
- authority cannot be obtained;
- resources are unavailable;
- a better path emerges.

Programmes can be stopped.

---

## 74. Sunk-Cost Doctrine

Aurex should not continue a programme merely because time, money, reputation or effort has already been invested.

```text
PAST INVESTMENT
      ≠
FUTURE JUSTIFICATION
```

A failed or stopped programme can still create institutional learning.

---

## 75. Programme Closure

Programme closure should document:

- reason for closure;
- final maturity level;
- outcomes achieved;
- evidence produced;
- unresolved risks;
- assets or records retained;
- stakeholder communication;
- lessons learned;
- follow-on decisions.

Closure should be deliberate, not silent abandonment.

---

## 76. Programme Evaluation

Evaluation should compare actual results against the programme's objectives, evidence gates and success criteria.

Evaluation should distinguish:

```text
WHAT WAS BUILT
WHAT CHANGED
WHAT WAS PROVEN
WHAT WAS DISPROVEN
WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN
```

This supports honest learning.

---

## 77. Learning Loop

Programme learning should feed back into:

- research questions;
- architecture decisions;
- risk controls;
- partnership strategy;
- funding strategy;
- operating model;
- future programme concepts.

Aurex should preserve negative findings and failed assumptions as usable knowledge.

---

## 78. Flagship Programme

Aurex may eventually designate a flagship programme where evidence, mission alignment and institutional capacity justify that status.

A flagship label should not imply:

- government endorsement;
- utility endorsement;
- regulatory authority;
- national deployment;
- production maturity;
- asset ownership;
- system-operation authority.

Flagship status should increase governance discipline, not reduce it.

---

## 79. National DERMS as Programme and Governance Boundary

The National DERMS must remain a programme concept or evolving programme architecture until evidence, authority and institutional legitimacy justify stronger status.

Aurex should not represent National DERMS as:

- deployed national infrastructure;
- an authorized national programme;
- a regulatory function;
- a utility function;
- a national control system;
- an approved government platform;
- an operational system-of-record.

National-scale language requires national-scale evidence and authority.

---

## 80. Facility EMS Programme and Gate

Facility EMS may be the appropriate first programme or programme workstream because it can validate visibility, interoperability and local coordination at a bounded site level.

Before Facility EMS becomes a real pilot, Aurex should have:

- site authority;
- asset-owner permission;
- data rights;
- control boundaries;
- safety plan;
- security plan;
- baseline measurements;
- success criteria;
- support arrangement;
- exit condition.

Facility EMS should not be described as production merely because it functions in a test environment.

---

## 81. Regional DERMS Programme

Regional DERMS should be considered only after multi-facility validation creates credible evidence that coordination across facilities can produce useful outcomes.

A regional programme would require additional authority, stakeholder coordination, data governance, security, operational support and regulatory clarity.

It should not be treated as an automatic consequence of Facility EMS progress.

---

## 82. National Programme Progression

Aurex's national-scale progression should remain evidence-gated:

```text
FACILITY PROGRAMME

MULTI-FACILITY VALIDATION

REGIONAL PROGRAMME

INSTITUTIONAL COLLABORATION

NATIONAL PROGRAMME RESEARCH

AUTHORIZED NATIONAL PROGRAMME
```

There are no automatic arrows between these stages.

Each stage requires its own evidence, authority, capacity, risk review and institutional legitimacy.

---

## 83. Energy Operating System Programme Boundary

The Energy Operating System remains a long-term institutional and architectural vision unless evidence establishes a programme case.

Aurex should not collapse:

```text
VISION
  ≠
PROGRAMME CASE
  ≠
AUTHORIZED PROGRAMME
  ≠
OPERATIONAL INFRASTRUCTURE
```

The vision can guide research and architecture without becoming a current authority claim.

---

## 84. Portfolio Governance

As multiple programme concepts emerge, Aurex should govern them as a portfolio.

Portfolio governance asks:

- which programmes best serve the mission;
- which are evidence-ready;
- which are affordable;
- which risks are acceptable;
- which dependencies are critical;
- which work should stop or wait.

Not every good idea should become active work at the same time.

---

## 85. Prioritization

Programme prioritization should consider:

- mission relevance;
- problem evidence;
- stakeholder value;
- technical feasibility;
- authority requirements;
- risk;
- funding;
- capacity;
- learning value;
- public-interest effect.

Prioritization should be explicit where resources are limited.

---

## 86. Capacity and Overload

Aurex should not launch more programmes than it can govern.

Overload creates:

- weak assurance;
- poor delivery;
- hidden risk;
- shallow research;
- inaccurate claims;
- founder bottlenecks;
- stakeholder disappointment.

Capacity is a governance constraint.

---

## 87. Partnerships in Programmes

Programme partnerships should define:

- purpose;
- contribution;
- authority;
- data rights;
- asset rights;
- public communication rules;
- funding terms;
- IP or licensing boundaries;
- risk allocation;
- exit terms.

A partnership should not silently create programme authority beyond the agreed relationship.

---

## 88. Government and Public-Institution Relationships

Engagement with government or public institutions should be represented accurately.

Aurex should distinguish:

```text
DISCUSSION
ENGAGEMENT
LETTER OF SUPPORT
MEMORANDUM OF UNDERSTANDING
CONTRACT
REGULATORY APPROVAL
FORMAL PROGRAMME AUTHORIZATION
```

These are not equivalent.

---

## 89. Utility and Asset-Owner Relationships

Utility, facility and asset-owner relationships should distinguish:

- access to information;
- access to site;
- access to data;
- permission to integrate;
- permission to monitor;
- permission to control;
- operational responsibility;
- ongoing support responsibility.

Control authority must be explicit.

---

## 90. Programme Communications

Programme communications should be truthful about:

- maturity;
- evidence;
- authority;
- partnerships;
- deployment status;
- geography;
- operational capability;
- limitations.

Aurex should not let strategic ambition become public misrepresentation.

---

## 91. Government Claims

Aurex should not claim government authorization, endorsement, procurement, partnership or national mandate unless such status exists in a clear, documented and current form.

The existence of a public-interest mission does not create public authority.

---

## 92. Programme Naming

Programme names should not overstate maturity or authority.

A name such as National DERMS may describe an architectural ambition or programme concept only if communications also make the maturity and authority boundary clear.

Names should not cause stakeholders to believe Aurex operates or controls infrastructure that it does not operate or control.

---

## 93. AI and Automation in Programmes

AI and automation may support programme work, but they require governance.

Programme use of AI or automation should address:

- purpose;
- data access;
- model limitations;
- human oversight;
- explainability where needed;
- safety constraints;
- security;
- auditability;
- failure behavior.

Automation should not silently acquire decision authority.

---

## 94. Maturity Review

Programme maturity should be reviewed when:

- entering a programme;
- passing a gate;
- expanding scope;
- moving to pilot;
- seeking deployment;
- making public claims;
- accepting major funding;
- adding material partners;
- transitioning to operations.

Maturity labels should describe reality.

---

## 95. Programme Register

Aurex should progressively maintain a programme register.

A register may include:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Programme ID | Traceability |
| Name | Programme identity |
| Status | Current state |
| Maturity | PG0-PG7 internal maturity |
| Owner | Accountable person or current founder-led ownership |
| Objective | Outcome sought |
| Scope | Boundary |
| Key evidence | Why it exists |
| Key risks | Material uncertainty |
| Next gate | Required decision |

The register should not invent active programmes where only concepts exist.

Programme IDs should support traceability.

They may become useful when Aurex manages multiple programmes, workstreams, pilots and evidence gates.

IDs should be administrative tools, not a substitute for governance substance.

It is not proof of operational programme maturity.

## 99. Programme Decision Test

Before Aurex calls something a programme, it should ask:

```text
IS THE PROBLEM VALIDATED?
IS THE OUTCOME DEFINED?
IS THE EVIDENCE SUFFICIENT?
IS THERE ONE ACCOUNTABLE OWNER?
IS AUTHORITY EXPLICIT?
IS THE SCOPE CONTROLLED?
ARE RISKS VISIBLE?
ARE RESOURCES PLAUSIBLE?
ARE GATES DEFINED?
CAN THE PROGRAMME BE STOPPED?
```

If the answer is no, the work may still be research, concept development or a project.

---

## 100. Programme Governance Equation

Aurex programme governance should satisfy:

```text
VALIDATED PROBLEM
+
DEFINED OUTCOME
+
ACCOUNTABLE OWNER
+
EXPLICIT AUTHORITY
+
CONTROLLED SCOPE
+
RESOURCES
+
RISK CONTROL
+
EVIDENCE GATES
+
ASSURANCE
+
HONEST COMMUNICATION
=
GOVERNED PROGRAMME
```

Weakness in one part should trigger review rather than concealment.
